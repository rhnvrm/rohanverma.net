> **About this essay:** This essay grew out of a conversation between ChatGPT and me. ChatGPT wrote it as a summary of our discussion, with editing and reference checks for this page. It explores how language conveys requests, politeness, and shared responsibility. The proposed comparison between Indian and German-influenced English, and the connection to how people prompt AI agents, are research questions rather than established findings.

## Why does "let us" sound different from "let's"?

"Let's do this" is an ordinary suggestion. Expand it to "let us do this," and the sentence can sound formal, ceremonial, or like a request for permission. The words seem equivalent on paper, but substituting one for the other changes how the utterance is heard.

The difference is not absolute. "Let us pray" is an invitation to joint action, and "let us consider the evidence" is familiar in formal speech. The full form has not lost its suggestive use. Nevertheless, in casual conversation, "let's" is the conventional form for many proposals involving the speaker. Cambridge's grammar describes both the suggestion use and its relationship to the full form.[^cambridge]

This was the starting point of the conversation: how can a contraction acquire a use that its expanded form no longer carries quite as naturally?

## More than a shorter pronunciation

One way to understand the difference is through grammaticalization: expressions with more independent lexical meanings develop conventional grammatical functions. Paul Hopper and Elizabeth Closs Traugott describe this broader process in *Grammaticalization*.[^grammaticalization]

The verb *let* can mean allow or permit. In "let us out," someone is being asked to allow us to leave. But "let's go out" ordinarily proposes an action. The contraction participates in a construction with its own familiar use, often called a hortative: an encouragement or proposal to act.

That distinction is visible in what can be substituted. "Let us out" cannot simply become "let's out." Knowing what the contraction historically contains is not enough to predict where it works today.

A plausible explanation is that frequent use, reduced pronunciation, and repeated interpretation reinforced one another. A shorter form became familiar in proposals, and listeners learned to recognize that function. The unreduced expression remained available for permission requests and more formal suggestions.

This is an account to investigate, not a reconstruction established by the examples alone. Demonstrating the historical sequence would require dated evidence of how both forms were used. Their present-day difference does not prove that speakers deliberately created it to resolve ambiguity.

The distinction can help listeners disambiguate, even if disambiguation was never anyone's conscious plan.

## A question can ask someone to act

The discussion then moved from "let's" to "can we."

"Can we add tests here?" has the grammatical form of a question about possibility. In a code review, it may function as a request to add tests. The reviewer could be asking whether tests are feasible, proposing that both people investigate, or expecting the author to make a change. The sentence does not determine which interpretation applies by itself.

John Searle's account of indirect speech acts examines how an utterance can perform an action beyond the one suggested by its literal form.[^searle] A question about ability can also request action because speakers share conventions and enough context to infer what is wanted.

This is why answering only "yes" can fail to satisfy "can you close the window?" The listener has answered the literal question while leaving the request unfulfilled.

In workplace language, similar interpretations arise with "can we check this once?" or "could we move this to tomorrow?" Sometimes a decision is open. Sometimes the phrasing softens an instruction whose intended outcome is already fairly clear. To understand the utterance, we need to know who is speaking, who can act, and what has already been agreed.

## Who is included in "we"?

"Let's fix this" presents an action as shared. That can describe the work accurately: two people might sit down and debug together. It can also frame work as collective when only the listener is expected to carry it out.

A manager saying "let's get this deployed today" may be offering help, setting a team goal, or assigning a task. The pronoun does not settle who will do the deployment. It presents a relationship between the speaker, the listener, and the proposed work.

"Can we" adds another possibility. It can invite agreement while leaving the executor unnamed. "We should" can present the action as something the situation requires, allowing the speaker to recommend it without issuing an explicit order.

These are possible interpretations, not fixed translations. "Please fix this" can be friendly and appropriate. "Could we perhaps fix this?" can sound impatient after the fifth reminder. A softened grammatical form does not guarantee a gentle interaction.

The question is how people learn which interpretation is ordinary in their setting, and what happens when their expectations differ.

## Politeness involves more than adding "please"

Brown and Levinson's influential account of politeness describes requests partly through their effects on the listener's freedom to act and their relationship with the speaker.[^politeness] A request asks someone to spend time, change plans, or accept an obligation. Speakers can acknowledge that imposition, soften it, or emphasize a shared purpose.

"Could you check this?" can leave apparent room to decline. "Let's check this" can emphasize affiliation. Both can help manage a relationship, but they do so differently.

This theory offers a vocabulary for analyzing the examples. It does not give us a universal scale on which every question is more polite than every imperative. Local expectations matter. A direct instruction during an incident may be welcome because it identifies an action clearly. In another setting, the same instruction may imply authority the speaker does not have.

Politeness depends on the participants' understanding of the situation as well as the words they use.

## What can we say about Indian English?

The conversation raised a more specific possibility: are inclusive forms such as "let's," "can we," and "we should" especially conventional in Indian English?

There is research on request patterns in Indian English, but the scope of each study matters. Julia Degenhardt's *Requests in Indian and Sri Lankan English* compares those varieties with British English using spoken material from the International Corpus of English. It reports quantitative differences in how requests are realized and uses a multifactorial analysis.[^degenhardt]

That provides a basis for asking about local conventions. It does not, by itself, establish how frequently Indian software engineers say "can we" in code reviews, or whether they use it more than German speakers writing in English. The comparison, participants, and communicative setting would all be different.

Variation within India matters too. Thomas Tinkham's earlier study of sociocultural variation in Indian English examined differences in directive forms using literary dialogue as evidence. Its categories and source material limit what can be inferred about contemporary speech, but it illustrates why treating Indian English as a single uniform request style would be inadequate.[^tinkham]

A defensible hypothesis is that some Indian English workplace communities have conventionalized inclusive and interrogative forms as ordinary ways to direct work. Whether those forms occur more frequently than elsewhere, and under which conditions, remains something to measure.

## Why the German comparison needs care

English and German communicative styles have been compared in pragmatics research, including work by Juliane House.[^house] That makes the comparison a reasonable research direction. It does not allow us to take an observation about Indian English and an observation about German, combine them, and conclude how the two populations behave in the same English-speaking workplace.

Speaking German, speaking English as an additional language, and writing a review comment in an international engineering team are different situations. The comparison needs to specify which one is being studied.

If we compare an Indian colleague's peer request with a German manager's urgent instruction, differences in authority and urgency could explain the wording. Company norms, familiarity, professional role, and proficiency in English could also affect it.

The research question becomes more precise when those conditions are specified: in comparable workplace situations, do speakers from different linguistic backgrounds choose different request forms, and do listeners interpret them differently?

A difference in interpretation may matter even if the forms occur at similar rates. One reader may take "can we add tests?" as a clear request to do so. Another may hear an invitation to discuss whether tests are needed. Neither interpretation can be assigned to a nationality without evidence.

## What would count as evidence?

A study could examine comparable workplace exchanges, using material collected with permission and appropriate privacy protections. It would need enough surrounding conversation to distinguish a question about feasibility from a request to act.

Each request could be annotated for its grammatical form, who is addressed, who is expected to perform the work, the relationship between participants, and urgency. Researchers would also need to distinguish actual joint work from work presented as joint through "we."

The next reply would be useful evidence. Did the listener answer a question, accept a task, ask who should do it, or begin discussing alternatives? The response can reveal how the utterance functioned in that exchange.

A complementary experiment could hold the situation constant while changing the wording. Participants could interpret several versions of the same request and identify the expected executor, whether action is required, and whether disagreement seems possible. Ratings of politeness and clarity would add another view.

Corpus data would show what people use. Interpretation studies would help establish what listeners understand. Together they could test the proposed differences without assuming that national labels explain every interaction.

## The same habits in prompts for agents

The discussion eventually returned to a familiar setting: asking an AI agent to do work.

People write "let's debug this," "can we add tests," and "we should simplify this function" to systems that could also receive a direct instruction. These expressions may carry habits from ordinary workplace interaction into a new setting.

Research by Clifford Nass, Jonathan Steuer, and Ellen Tauber on computers as social actors provides an earlier basis for studying people's social responses to computers.[^computers] It does not establish this particular claim about Indian English or present-day coding agents. The proposed transfer of request conventions is a further hypothesis.

There are at least two separate things to investigate. One is why users choose a form: habit, courtesy, a preferred working relationship, or a deliberate invitation to discuss. The other is what an agent does with it.

For example, if "can we add tests?" is intended as a request, an agent that only explains how tests could be added has misunderstood the expected action. But if the user is asking whether testing is feasible, immediately editing files may also miss the point. A useful interpretation depends on the surrounding conversation and the work already authorized.

We could test whether wording changes an agent's behavior by comparing otherwise equivalent prompts. Separately, we could study which forms different users choose and what they intend. Evidence about user habits would not automatically establish an effect on the model, and a model's response would not explain why the user chose those words.

## What the small phrases carry

Like [*Bhāva and feeling*](@/pages/essays/bhava-and-feeling.md), this discussion asks what a familiar expression carries beyond the meanings of its component words. That essay asks how words organize descriptions of inner experience. Here, the question is how phrases organize action between people.

"Let's" can propose joint action. "Can we" can ask for work while leaving responsibility implicit. Listeners learn to interpret such expressions through repeated interactions, and those expectations may follow them into conversations with agents.

The contraction that started the discussion therefore leads to a larger empirical question: when someone phrases an instruction as something "we" might do, who understands themselves to be responsible, and what happens next?
